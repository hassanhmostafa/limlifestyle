Key Recommendations 
This guideline provides evidence-based recommendations to prevent, manage, and 
potentially reverse chronic lifestyle-related diseases aligned with Saudi Vision 2030 goals to 
improve population health and quality of life.
A. GENERAL PRINCIPLES
−Patient-Centered Care: Develop personalized plans based on individual health status, 
readiness to change, and cultural context, with scheduled regular follow-ups.
−Multidisciplinary Approach: Engage primary care physicians, dietitians, psychologists, 
and other professionals.
−Behavioral Change Frameworks: Use validated models, such as Motivational 
Interviewing (MI) and Transtheoretical Model (TTM) to enhance adherence.
−Individualize prescriptions: Use the SMART format (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, 
Relevant, Time-bound) to set and monitor goals.
B. PILLARS OF LIFESTYLE MEDICINE 
1. Nutrition Recommendations:
−Encourage a whole-food, plant-predominant diet emphasizing vegetables, fruits, 
legumes, whole grains, nuts, and seeds.
−Limit processed foods, added sugars, saturated and trans fats, and excessive sodium.
−Recommend Mediterranean or Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet 
for cardiovascular and metabolic health. The Portfolio Diet may help further reduce 
low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol levels.
−For individuals with obesity, set realistic caloric deficits tailored to their needs.
2. Physical Activity Recommendations:
−Adults: Engage in ≥150 minutes/week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity or ≥75 
minutes/week of vigorous activity. Include resistance training 2–3 times/week, plus 
flexibility and balance exercises 2–3 times/week. Avoid prolonged sitting.
−Tailor physical activity plans for chronic conditions (e.g., obesity, diabetes, 
hypertension, asthma, Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), and cardiac 
disease), ensuring individualized and progressive regimens.
−Conduct medical evaluation (e.g., PAR-Q+) before vigorous activity in high-risk 
individuals.

4.2. Physical Activity Science and Prescription
4.2.5 PHYSICAL ACTIVITY GUIDELINES
A. Components of an Exercise Prescription for Enhancing Aerobic and Muscular 
Fitness:
An individualized exercise program should include the following essential components:
– Warm-Up:
This phase consists of a minimum of 10 to 15 minutes of low to moderate-intensity 
cardiovascular (aerobic) and muscular endurance activity designed to increase body 
temperature. The purpose of a good warm-up is to gradually raise the heart rate and core 
temperature to the prescribed exercise intensity. The warm-up phase improves the 
mechanical efficiency of the circulatory system by decreasing the viscosity of joint fluids 
and inducing vasodilation of muscle vessels, making it easier for the heart to pump blood 
throughout the body.
– Pre-Exercise Stretching:
This phase should focus on range of motion exercises that target the joints preferentially 
used in the conditioning phase. For example, if swimming is the exercise mode, range of 
motion exercises for the shoulders, neck, and hips should be included.
– Conditioning (Stimulus Phase):
This phase consists of the main part of a workout session where the body is actively 
challenged to improve aerobic endurance and/or muscular strength and fitness.
– Cool-Down:
The cool-down phase is a structured period at the end of exercise designed to gradually 
return the body to its resting state. It typically lasts 5–10 minutes and includes low-intensity 
aerobic activity (e.g., walking, slow cycling) followed by static stretching. This phase 
facilitates gradual recovery, prevents venous pooling, and reduces post-exercise 
dizziness.

4.2.5 PHYSICAL ACTIVITY GUIDELINES
C. FITT Principles for Resistance Training (ACSM Guidelines):
1. Frequency: Two to three non-consecutive days each week
2. Intensity: Older adults and beginners should start with lighter intensity (40–50% 1RM) 
before progressing. Intermediate exercise should use roughly 60%–80% 1RM. 
Experienced resistance trainers can use 80% of their 1RM to improve strength. Lighter 
loads with shorter rest periods can improve endurance.
3. Time: No recommended duration.
4. Type: It is optimal to include a variety of exercises targeting multiple joints and major 
muscle groups. Mixing different types of equipment is also beneficial. Target both agonist 
and antagonist muscle groups.
5. Reps: Most adults should do 8–12 reps, with older adults starting at 10–15 with a 
lighter weight. Those wishing to improve endurance should do 10–15 reps.
6. Sets: Two to four sets are optimal, although novices may benefit from a single set.
7. Rest: Rest for a couple of minutes between sets. Give major muscle groups 48 hours 
of recovery between sessions.
8. Progression: A gradual progression of resistance, repetition, and frequency is 
recommended.

Safety Considerations for Exercise in Patients with Obesity:
1. Individualization: Develop exercise prescriptions tailored to each patient’s specific 
comorbidities and overall health status.
2. Progression: Gradual Aerobic Engagement: Initiate with low-intensity aerobic activities 
and progressively increase both duration and intensity as the patient's fitness level 
improves.
3. Joint protection: Favor low-impact or non–weight-bearing activities to minimize stress 
on the knees and joints, thereby reducing the risk of injury.
4. Safety precautions: Instruct patients to cease activity immediately if they experience 
symptoms such as chest pain, shortness of breath, or significant muscle or joint pain. 
5. Caution in severe obesity:  Vigorous exercise may not be suitable for individuals with 
severe obesity (BMI ≥ 35) due to an increased risk of orthopedic injuries.

4.3. Sleep Health and Intervention
4.3.4.2 SLEEP ENVIRONMENT
−For optimal sleep, advise patients to create a dark, cool (but not cold), and quiet (not 
silent) bedroom.
−The body’s ability to regulate temperature decreases during sleep; encourage a 
comfortable thermal environment.
−Recommend removing TVs, computers, and smartphones from the bedroom to 
minimize distractions.
−Suggest warm baths or showers before bed, wearing socks, and reducing evening 
caffeine intake for patients with cold extremities.
−Recommend cooling methods such as fans, cooling mats, or lowering the room 
temperature for patients with elevated temperatures near bedtime
−White noise (e.g., fan, machine) may help mask disruptive sounds.
−Reduce nighttime light exposure by repositioning light sources, using blackout 
curtains, or wearing sleep masks.
−Increase morning light exposure immediately upon waking.
−Recommend table lamps over overhead lighting, closing windows and blinds, and 
using blue light-blocking glasses in the evening.
−Counsel patients to reserve their bed for sleep and intimacy, moving other activities out 
of the bedroom.
− Emphasize the importance of minimizing light exposure and establishing a calming 
bedtime routine, including ceasing stimulating activities 60–90 minutes before bed in 
favor of relaxing ones.
−For patients with nighttime rumination, recommend journaling or preparing a to-do list 
earlier in the evening to reduce cognitive arousal at bedtime.

4.3. Sleep Health and Intervention
4.3.4.3 WEIGHT REDUCTION
−Encouraging healthy weight reduction in individuals with overweight and obesity can 
improve sleep quality and reduce symptoms of sleep-related disorders such as OSA, 
RLS, and parasomnias.
−Adopting good sleep practices can support weight loss efforts.
4.3.4.4 NUTRITION
−Avoid large meals 2–3 hours before bedtime to minimize gastrointestinal discomfort.
−Limit evening snacks, especially high-fat or spicy foods.
−Ensure adequate hydration throughout the day; avoid excess fluids close to bedtime.
4.3.4.5 EXERCISE
−Encourage patients to increase daily physical activity, aiming for 150 minutes of 
moderate to high-intensity exercise per week to support circadian regulation.
−Avoid vigorous exercise late in the evening, as it may delay sleep onset.
4.3.4.6 STRESS
−Inform patients that both sleep quality and quantity are significantly impacted by acute 
and chronic stress.
−Encourage stress management strategies to improve sleep.
4.3.4.7 JET LAG
−Traveling East: Encourage bright light exposure outdoors shortly before the new 
wake- up time, limit dim light exposure one hour before the new sleep time and 
suggest a substantial breakfast within 30–45 minutes of waking. Melatonin may aid in 
adjustment.
−Traveling West: Recommend bright light exposure in the late afternoon and early 
evening, avoid dim light until one hour before the new sleep time, and advise a large 
breakfast 30–45 minutes after waking. A carbohydrate-rich dinner should be consumed 
two to three hours before the new sleep time, with melatonin supplements considered 
for smoother adaptation.

4.3. Sleep Health and Intervention
4.3.5 PRESCRIPTION
Sleep is a fundamental component of a healthy lifestyle, essential for both short- and long-
term health. Inadequate sleep is linked to chronic conditions such as obesity, diabetes, 
cardiovascular disease, and impairments in cognitive function, emotional regulation, and 
mental resilience. Lifestyle medicine provides evidence-based sleep prescriptions tailored 
to individual needs, which should adhere to the SMART criteria: Specific, Measurable, 
Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound.
4.3.5.1 LIFESTYLE MEDICINE PRESCRIPTIONS TO OPTIMIZE SLEEP HEALTH
– Aim for 7–9 hours of sleep per night.
– Maintain a consistent sleep–wake schedule, including non-workdays.
– Relaxing bedtime routine.
– Comfortable sleep environment.
– Remove electronic devices from the bedroom.
– Regular exercise.
– Avoid stimulants in the afternoon.
– Limit naps.
– Avoid large meals within 2–3 hours of bedtime.
–  If you are having difficulty falling asleep, get out of bed.
–  Decrease weight for overweight and obese patients.
–  Deal with stressors.
–  Follow up regularly and adjust recommendations based on patient progress.
–  Referral indication in section 4.3.6.3.

4.5.7 OTHER FORMS OF TOBACCO USE AND EXPOSURE
C. Secondhand Smoke and Its Health Impacts
Secondhand smoke, also known as passive smoking, can adversely affect the health of 
non-smokers. Evidence indicates that exposure to environmental tobacco smoke can lead 
to significant health risks for different groups:
• Pregnancy: Exposure during pregnancy is associated with adverse outcomes, 
including low birth weight and preterm delivery.
• Children: Children exposed to secondhand smoke face higher rates of respiratory 
infections, middle ear infections, meningococcal infections, and bronchial asthma
• Adults: Secondhand smoke exposure increases the risk of lung cancer, coronary 
heart disease, and stroke.
Health authorities, including the National Health and Medical Research Council, have 
summarized the health effects of secondhand smoke. The U.S. Department of Health and 
Human Services has asserted that there is no safe level of exposure to tobacco smoke. 
Even minimal exposure, such as occasional cigarettes or secondhand smoke, can be 
harmful, particularly to children.36

4.6 Social Connectedness
4.6.1 INTRODUCTION 
Meaningful connections enhance emotional resilience, mental health, physical well-being, 
and overall life satisfaction. This section will discuss the impact of social connectedness 
and social networks, supported by key research findings and evidence-based 
recommendations. It aims to help healthcare providers recognize the clinical relevance of 
social connectedness and intentionally foster it in patients’ lives during counseling.1
4.6.2 KEY EVIDENCE & FINDINGS
– The Harvard Study of Adult Development (75-year cohort) identified social connections 
as the strongest predictor of happiness and longevity.
– Positive emotional states spread through social networks up to three degrees of 
separation (friends of friends’ friends), influencing health behaviors and outcomes.
– Social isolation has profound negative impacts on mental and physical health.
– Neuroimaging shows that social isolation activates brain regions associated with 
physical pain, increased risk of depression, anxiety, and chronic illness.
– Positivity resonance and micro-moments of connection improve health outcomes.
– Positive interactions characterized by shared emotions, empathy, and care enhance 
physiological measures such as heart-rate variability.
– Even brief authentic social interactions provide cumulative emotional and health 
benefits.42-44
